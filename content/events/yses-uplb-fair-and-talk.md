## The day job, for once

Almost everything on this site that involves students involves me teaching them. This was the other
one. [Career Team](https://careerteam.com) is my full-time work, where I am a technical lead, and the
teaching is the part-time half of the week even though it is the half people ask about. On this day
the full-time job was the one holding the microphone.

Two slots, same campus, same afternoon. A booth at the Practicum Fair and Job Fair, then a stage at
Company Talks. Both of them are recruiting. Saying so up front is cheaper than letting a reader work
it out and wonder what else was quietly reframed.

## 130 names, and what that number is not

That is the count off the booth sheet. It excludes the students who handed over a resume and walked
on, and the ones who scanned the QR code for the job postings and thought about it later. So 130 is
the floor and I have no idea what the ceiling was.

It is also not a result. 130 people wrote their names down. That is not 130 interviews and it is
certainly not 130 jobs, and any recruiting number quoted without that caveat is being asked to do a
job it cannot do.

Five of us behind one table, all team leads: Christelle Salceda,
[Jun Bolotaolo](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jun-welmer-bolotaolo-b683a01a4/),
[Beili Cepe](https://www.linkedin.com/in/claizel-coubeili/),
[Jake Regunay](https://www.linkedin.com/in/aifel-jake-regunay-690666270/), and me. Students came
from across the region, not only the host campus. Almost none of them asked about pay.
They asked whether we take interns and what they were expected to already know, which are better
questions than the ones I was asking at their age.

## The slide I actually stand behind

The talk was called From Code to Career: Inside a Modern Tech Team. The deck is at the top of this
page, so you can check me against it rather than take my summary.

One slide is the reason I was glad to give it:

> We aren't looking for "AI-prompt engineers". We are looking for developers who are already smart.
> We hire for foundational logic and the ability to solve complex problems from first principles.

Next to it, AI as a multiplier and not a crutch: use it to move faster, do not lean on it to find the
solution. And above both, the claim that hiring is going up rather than down as these tools arrive.

Full disclosure, since it would be easy to read that slide as somebody else backing me up: I wrote
the deck. It is the same argument I make at length in [ten times zero](/notes/ten-times-zero), put in
front of the people it is actually about.

What is not mine is the sign-off. A company agreed to say that to a room it recruits from, which
turns an opinion into something a candidate can hold it to. And it holds in exactly the room where a
student most wants it to be false, because from a lecture hall the shortcut looks extremely
available.

## The company, for anyone deciding whether to apply

Career Team builds workforce software: the systems that sit between a person who needs work and a
program built to help them find it. Since 1996, mostly for the United States. The platform is Career
Edge. The deck's own figures are over a million job seekers served, more than five hundred campuses
and forty-three workforce centers, and I am repeating those as the company's numbers rather than
presenting them as something I measured.

The stack is MERN with Next.js, which for this room was the useful detail: the same tools they are
being taught, running in production instead of in a course project.

The closing advice slide has three items. Build in public. Stay curious. And do not chase every new
framework, learn JavaScript deeply, build a foundation that lasts decades rather than months. That
last one landed on the screen almost exactly a year after
[I spent an afternoon teaching a room a new framework](/calendar/codegeeks-hau-sleek-and-swift).
Both are true at once. The framework gets you past the screening; the foundation is what you still
have when that framework becomes somebody's migration ticket.

## The honest limits

I was selling. Every line in that deck about mentoring, about interns shipping production code from
day one, about the no stupid questions policy, is a company describing itself. I believe it or I
would not have stood up with it, but a talk is not evidence, and a student should treat the whole
thing as a list of questions to ask rather than a set of answers.

I also had a microphone, a stage and nobody with a rebuttal. Comfortable place to be right from.

Thank you to [YSES](https://yses.org), the Young Software Engineers' Society at UP Los Baños, for
having us. EXPE21ENCE was their twenty-first year of this, and it is not a small thing: a job fair, a
hackfest and a day of company talks in one stretch, with AWS Philippines and Accenture in the other
speaking slots, sponsors to wrangle, a venue to fill and a schedule to hold. The officers did all of
it between their own classes, and the only reason I got a stage and a table full of students to talk
to is that they built both.
