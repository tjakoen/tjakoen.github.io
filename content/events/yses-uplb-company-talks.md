## Say what the talk was

A recruiting talk. A company sends people to a job fair, the people stand on a stage, and the point
is that some of the students in the room apply. Pretending it was anything loftier would be the exact
kind of thing this site is supposed to not do.

Inside that frame there was a real argument, and it is the reason I was happy to give it.

## The slide I actually stand behind

The deck at the top of this page has a slide called AI Tools in Software Development. Three claims on
it, and I will quote the one that matters:

> We aren't looking for "AI-prompt engineers". We are looking for developers who are already smart.
> We hire for foundational logic and the ability to solve complex problems from first principles.

Next to it: AI as a multiplier, not a crutch. Use it to move faster, do not rely on it to find the
solution. And above both, the line that hiring is going up rather than down with the arrival of these
tools.

I have been arguing that for a while now, at length, in
[ten times zero](/notes/ten-times-zero). Finding it in a corporate recruiting deck was a strange kind
of confirmation. The argument holds whoever is holding the microphone, and it holds in exactly the
room where a student most wants it to be false, because the shortcut looks so available from there.

## The company, for anyone deciding whether to apply

[Career Team](https://careerteam.com) builds workforce software, the systems that sit between a
person who needs work and a program built to help them find it. Since 1996, mostly for the United
States. The platform is Career Edge. The deck's own figures: over a million job seekers served, more
than five hundred campuses, forty-three workforce centers. Those are the company's numbers, and I am
repeating them as such rather than presenting them as something I measured.

The stack is MERN with Next.js, which for a room of computer science students is the useful part:
that is the same set of tools they are already being taught, running in production rather than in a
course project.

## What the students should have taken from it

The last content slide is three pieces of advice, and the middle one has aged strangely for me.
Build in public, share the wins and the struggles. Stay curious. And: do not chase every new
framework, learn JavaScript deeply, build a foundation that lasts decades rather than months.

That last one arrived on a slide almost exactly a year after
[I spent an afternoon teaching a room a new framework](/calendar/codegeeks-hau-sleek-and-swift).
Both are true. The framework gets you through the interview screen; the foundation is what you still
have when that framework is a legacy migration.

The one line I would put above all of it, and it is the closing note of that slide: technical skill
gets you the interview, communication and problem-solving get you the job.

## The honest limits

I was selling. Every claim in that deck about mentoring, about interns shipping production code from
day one, about the no stupid questions policy, is the company describing itself. I believe it or I
would not have stood up with it, but a talk is not evidence and a student should treat it as a
starting point for questions rather than an answer.

I also had a stage, a microphone and no rebuttal. That is a comfortable place to be right from.

Company Talks was part of EXPE21ENCE, run by [YSES](https://yses.org), the Young Software Engineers'
Society at UP Los Baños, in their twenty-first year, with AWS Philippines and Accenture in the other
slots. The officers ran the room, handed over the certificate in the last photo, and did all of it
between their own classes. The booth we worked earlier the same day has
[its own post](/calendar/yses-uplb-job-fair).
