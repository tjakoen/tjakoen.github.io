Once an AI does the typing, something odd happens. You start having ideas faster than you can build them.
A thing that would've taken a month is an evening, so every annoyance becomes a possible project, and
the ideas turn up at the worst times: in the shower, on the bus, in the middle of a lecture about something else.

Most of them vanish. You tell yourself you'll remember and then it's Thursday and you remember that you had
an idea, and that's all. That's the first problem, and it has a one-line fix.

## Catch it in one line

Keep a single inbox somewhere you can reach from anywhere. It could be a notes app on your phone. It could be
a plain text file that syncs to your phone. Use whichever you'll actually open.

When an idea shows up, write one line: what it is, and the problem it solves, if you know. "A page that
shows which of my roadmap links have died." That's all. Don't design it yet, and don't open a harness. The
aim is just to get it out of your head so it can't be lost, and so you stop holding it.

Keep the inbox ugly and the lines short. A catch that takes longer than ten seconds won't get done on the bus.

## Then don't build from the line

This is the part I want you to hear, because it's the mistake a cheap build tempts you into. The line in
your inbox is a catch, not a brief. If you paste "a page that shows which of my roadmap links have died"
straight into an agent, you'll get something. It'll be confident and a bit beside the point, which is the
teammate from the first note.

So there's a step in between, and it's the one people skip. Pick one idea from the inbox, when you have time
for it, and turn it into a brief. The three parts you already know: the goal, what done looks like, and what to
leave alone. Your AI can help here. Tell it the line and ask it to interview you, one question at a time,
until the brief is complete. Then you edit it, because the edit is where your judgment goes in.

From there it's the path you've already walked. Have the AI draw what needs drawing, write a plan with a done
line per step, then build one step at a time and check each one. The inbox feeds the brief, the brief feeds
the plan, and the plan feeds the loop.

## Most ideas should stay in the inbox

An inbox that only grows is a guilt pile, so I'll say the hard thing: most of your ideas should never get
built. Looking at the list a week later, a lot of them will already look smaller. That's the inbox working.
Delete lines without ceremony, and treat the list as a place ideas are allowed to die cheaply.

A rule worth trying: one active project at a time, with one next step you could start in the next ten
minutes. Everything else waits in the inbox. When you're tempted to start a second thing, add a line instead.

If your inbox is big enough to need a board, I built that for myself. [PROOF](/proof) turns a folder of plans
into a live board, and [PANTRY](/pantry) keeps it open beside my work. They're mine, not requirements.
A text file is plenty to begin with, and I'd rather you start with the file.

## Your turn

For one day, write every idea down in one line, wherever you catch it. Don't judge them. The next evening,
pick the one that surprised you most and ask your AI to interview you into a three-part brief. Read it,
change one line, and save it next to your plan. You don't have to build it. Or keep the inbox going for a
week and tell me which idea you were glad to delete.

**Where I might be wrong.** A tidy capture habit can become its own way to procrastinate: a beautiful inbox and
nothing shipped. And some ideas are better chased the minute they show up, while you still feel them. My
rule of one active project is a preference, not a law, and plenty of people work well with three. Find
the number that doesn't leave you feeling behind.
