# Ten Times Zero Is Still Zero

Status: DRAFT script for Tjakoen to read aloud and edit. Cut into beats from the earlier narration; not yet read or checked by him.

How this script works. The note stays open on the page while the recording plays. Each beat is a short spoken line about one idea, and it names the phrase in the note that lights up while it plays. The wording is the earlier narration, cut to the lines that carry the idea. Format of a beat: a highlight line (section id, then a quote that must appear in that section of the note, or figure: and a figure name), then the spoken words.

## Stop 1: I was the zero

**Beat 1**
highlight: vibe-coder-is-used-as-an-insult | Prompt and pray. No tests, no idea, no receipts.

You know the tone people use for vibe coder. Someone who types a wish into a chat box, pastes whatever comes back, and ships a house of cards they could not explain if you held their coffee hostage. I call myself one anyway. I just add a word in front. Professional. And because professional is the kind of claim that should arrive with evidence, I did the one thing the label almost never does. I counted.

**Beat 2**
highlight: ai-is-a-multiplier-not-an-addend | And ten times zero is still zero.

AI does not add to what you can do. It multiplies it. And ten times zero is still zero. Give a strong developer an AI and you get a frighteningly fast strong developer. Give a beginner the same AI and you get a beginner who ships bugs faster and cannot tell which ones they are. Same tool. Wildly different results, because the tool multiplied what was already there. That is why just use AI is terrible advice for a beginner, and never use AI is terrible advice for a professional. The honest version sits in the middle, and it has an order of operations. Become worth multiplying first.

**Beat 3**
highlight: i-was-the-zero-for-years | I was a talented amateur

For most of my twenties I was the zero. I was a talented amateur with just enough instinct to talk my way into rooms I had no business being in. Coding in school came easy, so I coasted on easy. Say yes to running the thing before I actually knew how to run the thing. That is the trap. It worked just enough to keep me doing it, and never enough to get me what I was after. I do not regret a moment of it, because every one of those failures had the same thing in common. It was not the ideas. It was me. I kept starting things I was not the right person to start yet.

**Beat 4**
highlight: i-was-the-zero-for-years | You have to be the right person before you try the thing.

You have to be the right person before you try the thing. I paid the tuition so you do not have to. So I did the unglamorous thing. I stopped chasing the next launch and worked on the one variable I could fix, which was me. I went back and learned the foundations properly, not just enough to pass. That is what the word professional means to me. Not the title. The journey I had to take to be able to say any of this.

**Digest:** AI multiplies what you bring, so ten times zero is still zero. I was the zero for years. Become worth multiplying first.

**Trial:** Pick one thing you would hand to an AI this week. Write down honestly whether you could check its answer without it. If not, that is the foundation to lay first.

**Runes:**
ai-is-a-multiplier-not-an-addend | And ten times zero is still zero.
ai-is-a-multiplier-not-an-addend | become worth multiplying first

## Stop 2: A decade of rehearsal

**Beat 1**
highlight: so-were-clear-whos-talking | the typing was never the valuable part

I told you that backstory to buy one sentence. I could have written every line of this project by hand. A beginner vibe codes because they cannot write the code. I vibe code because I can. I have learned that the typing was never the valuable part. The value is knowing what to build, how it should be shaped, and catching the machine in the act when it has quietly done something dumb. I have made those same mistakes by hand, expensively, enough times to smell them coming. So when I hand the typing to a machine, I am not skipping the hard part. I did the hard part years ago, the boring way and the expensive way.

**Beat 2**
highlight: a-decade-of-rehearsal-billed-as-management | the skill my whole twenties

Here is what surprised me. Directing an AI was not a new skill I had to go and learn. It is the skill my whole twenties turned out to be building. The ventures that failed, the team I run, the classroom. I had been rehearsing for a decade without knowing what for. Think about what a dev manager does. You hand work to people whose hands you do not control, you give them enough context to succeed, and then you read what comes back and catch the problems before they ship. Now describe running an AI. It is the same list, top to bottom.

**Beat 3**
highlight: a-decade-of-rehearsal-billed-as-management | fast, capable, occasionally overconfident junior

So I stopped treating the model like a magic oracle or a fancy search box. I started treating it like what it actually resembles. A fast, capable, occasionally overconfident junior on my team. Or a student rushing through an activity just to get it done. The moment I framed it that way I got better at it overnight, because I already knew how to do that. Some evenings I have five to ten Claude sessions open at once, and it feels exactly like running a team. Delegate the work, keep each one productive, and stitch what comes out into something that works.

**Beat 4**
highlight: a-decade-of-rehearsal-billed-as-management | if you can't explain it, you didn't build it.

When I read what they produce, I ask the same questions I ask of a junior's pull request or a student's submission. Does this meet the objectives? Does it understand what it shipped? Is it in line with our standards and our architecture? And my one rule for students turns out to be my one rule for the machine. If you can't explain it, you didn't build it. I am not learning a new skill with AI. I am spending the one I already paid for.

**Digest:** I can write the code, which is why I let the machine type it. Running an AI uses the same skills as managing, leading and teaching. If you can't explain it, you didn't build it.

**Trial:** Write down one role you have already held, a job, a club, a group project, and list two moves from it that you could reuse when directing an AI.

**Runes:**
so-were-clear-whos-talking | the typing was never the valuable part
a-decade-of-rehearsal-billed-as-management | if you can't explain it, you didn't build it.

## Stop 3: The receipts

**Beat 1**
highlight: the-receipts | Not a demo, not a tutorial repo.

Everything in this stop comes straight from the git history of the thing you are reading this on. My portfolio, built on three tools I made for it. It is the thing I actually build on, and it is live. The main counts here were measured on the twelfth of November 2026, from committed files only, because those are the ones you can go and check. So here is the headline, because it reframes everything else. More than half of this repo is writing, not code. Seventy five thousand five hundred lines of words, roughly, against about fifty seven thousand lines of code.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-one-number-that-matters | the scarce resource stops being code

Sit with that, because it is the opposite of what vibe coding is supposed to look like. The caricature is a person who writes no docs, who could not produce a spec if you begged. This repo has the inverse problem. There is more specification than implementation. I did not tidy that up after the fact. It is the method. When you work with an AI at speed, the scarce resource stops being code and becomes intent. The model cannot know what you meant, what the rules are, or what done looks like, unless you wrote it down. So I write it down. The documentation is the work. The code is the cheap part the machine handles.

**Beat 3**
highlight: now-the-vibe-part-because-i-do-go-fast | with every commit. Last measured 2026-08-22: 503 commits

Now, if all that writing makes me sound slow and fussy, the tempo says otherwise. When I counted in August, this repo had 503 commits, and every single one was co-authored with an AI. Co-authored is the honest word. Two sets of hands on every commit. Across the seven repositories I keep, there were 1,246 commits between the twenty seventh of June and the twenty second of August. Eight weeks. One night in the middle of that, the fourth of July, I made fifty commits between nine in the evening and half past three in the morning, and added more than four thousand lines before it got light. One commit every eight minutes or so. That is vibe coding at full tilt.

**Beat 4**
highlight: now-the-vibe-part-because-i-do-go-fast | I built the rails before I opened the throttle

Each one is a thing that already works, and that is what makes the next one cheap to check. Fifty tiny commits you can verify beat five big ones you cannot. Fast and documented. The reason I can sprint all night and not wake up to a pile of nonsense is not that I am careful instead of fast. It is that I did the careful part first. I built the rails before I opened the throttle.

**Digest:** More than half of what I have written in this repo is words, not code. Writing down intent is the method. Speed is safe because the rails went up first.

**Trial:** Open a project of yours and roughly count how many files are writing (notes, a readme, docs) against code. Write down the split and one thing you wish you had written down.

## Stop 4: The playbook

**Beat 1**
highlight: the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | Professional vibe coding is structured delegation.

Amateur vibe coding is unstructured improvisation. Professional vibe coding is structured delegation. Same tools, same speed, wildly different amounts of scaffolding around the model. The AI can generate a thousand lines an hour. It cannot decide the layering rule, the token architecture, the single write door, or what done means. Those were mine. The machine typed. I engineered. I do not make the AI reliable by writing cleverer one-off prompts. I make it reliable by building it an environment where good work is the path of least resistance. That environment is a system, and you can copy it.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | Write the rails before the

Write the rails before the features. Before I built much of anything, I wrote the rulebook. You do not tell a fast driver to be careful. You build the track. Give the AI a memory. The model forgets everything between sessions, so I keep a running set of decision notes, about a hundred small records by now, of why things are the way they are. Next session the AI inherits the reasoning instead of relitigating it, or worse, quietly undoing it. Keep a sync map. The failure of fast AI work is usually not bad code. It is drift. You change one thing and six related things silently fall out of step. So I keep an explicit table: when you change this, also update that. It turns do not forget into a checklist a machine can follow.

**Beat 3**
highlight: the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | second pass has to be done by someone

Audit relentlessly. A model earns more scrutiny than a junior, not less, because it is fluent enough to sound right exactly when it is wrong. Most of my prompting is not build this. It is check this. Does it still match the goal? The second pass has to be done by someone who did not write the code. A session that just wrote something already believes it. It is the same reason you do not mark your own exam. Tests are part of the work, not a phase after it. The type checker and the test suite have to be green before I call anything done. It is the least glamorous rule and it does the most work. It is the seatbelt that lets me drive fast.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | A rule you have to remember is a wish.

Everything above is a habit, and habits are exactly what a tired person skips at two in the morning. I am the tired person. So the rules moved out of my head and into machinery. Every session opens with a checklist printed by a script, not recited by the model. Hooks refuse the moves I have already been burned by. A rule you have to remember is a wish. A rule that refuses the commit is a rail. I tried fixing bad answers with cleverer prompts for months. Then I stopped prompting harder and started leaving better notes.

**Digest:** Professional vibe coding is structured delegation. Seven rules build the environment, and the last one makes them enforce themselves. Brief, build, verify, record.

**Trial:** Open a cold AI session with no history, give it one sentence asking for a small change to a project of yours, and note what it guesses wrong first. That gap is a note you have not written yet.

**Runes:**
the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | Professional vibe coding is structured delegation.
the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | A rule you have to remember is a wish.
the-part-that-actually-makes-it-professional | I stopped prompting harder and started leaving better notes.

## Stop 5: The mistakes are a measurement

**Beat 1**
highlight: the-best-trick-of-all-the-ais-mistakes-are-a-measurement | So the mistakes stopped being bugs and started being data.

While I was building one of GRAIN's demo pages, I kept making small mistakes. The grain texture not showing on the AI's text. A chat that rendered as two stacked boxes instead of a conversation. Instead we stopped, the machine and I, and started asking questions. Why do I keep tripping on my own system? Am I the problem? Is the architecture I planned the problem? When the same mistake keeps coming back, you stop blaming the person making it and start suspecting the thing they are using. When half a class flubs the same question, the problem is the question, not the class. So the mistakes stopped being bugs and started being data.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-best-trick-of-all-the-ais-mistakes-are-a-measurement | single failure mode: silent-failure contracts.

Every one of them clustered in one failure mode, which I call silent-failure contracts. Mechanisms that quietly do nothing when you use them slightly wrong. No error, no warning. The documented way just does not take. That is about the most dangerous kind of design there is. A thing that fails loudly, you fix. A thing that fails silently, you ship. Nobody told it how everyone really does it. So when it trips on your system, that is not the machine being dumb. It is a free audit telling you that a tired human on a Friday will trip on exactly the same spot. Read the signal instead of just correcting the output.

**Beat 3**
highlight: the-best-trick-of-all-the-ais-mistakes-are-a-measurement | them later. We designed them

We designed them out. We made the grain work on any element, and shipped the chat as a container so messages cannot misalign. Can't be done wrong beats we'll catch it if it is. Then we backed that up with tests that check the design system is used correctly, not merely that the page renders. One of them proves that a human action and an AI action produce the exact same result. And every fix was proven against the real thing, with screenshots and real end-to-end runs, never it probably looks right. The one-sentence version. Where the AI keeps slipping is a map of where my design is not clear yet, and I harden the design so neither of us slips there again.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-tell-is-the-ratio | That surrounding structure is the profession.

An amateur and I could type the exact same request into the exact same model and get back the exact same code. The difference is not in that moment. It is in everything I built around it. The conventions the model read first, the memory it inherited, the tests it has to pass, the audit it has to survive. That surrounding structure is the profession. The code is just the code. It is the same law as the napkin at the start. The model multiplied what I brought.

**Digest:** When the AI keeps slipping in the same place, the design is unclear there. Design the mistake out, then lock it with a test. The structure around the model is the profession.

**Trial:** Think of one mistake you or an AI made twice in the same project. Write one sentence on what about the project made it easy to make, and one change that would make it impossible.

**Runes:**
the-best-trick-of-all-the-ais-mistakes-are-a-measurement | So the mistakes stopped being bugs and started being data.
the-tell-is-the-ratio | That surrounding structure is the profession.

## Stop 6: Run it, and where to start

**Beat 1**
highlight: dont-work-for-the-ai-run-it | accept nothing you couldn't defend with the tab closed

If you take one idea out of all of this, take this one, because it decides which of two developers you become. Working for the AI looks like this. You ask, it answers, you paste. The shape of the project ends up being whatever the machine happened to produce that afternoon. You decide what should exist. You cut it into pieces small enough to check in a minute each. You point the machine at one piece and read every line that comes back. And you accept nothing you couldn't defend with the tab closed.

**Beat 2**
highlight: where-to-start-if-youre-early | Pick a project you already

First, pick a project you already understand. Not a new one. You cannot judge a machine's output on a codebase you have never read, and judging the output is the entire skill. Second, write the brief before the feature. One file at the root: what this is, how it is built, what not to touch. Twenty minutes, once. Every session after it starts smarter. Third, turn on the cheap gates. Strict types, one test that actually runs, no empty catch blocks. All of that before lunch, and it keeps catching things all year.

**Beat 3**
highlight: where-to-start-if-youre-early | Every correction you give twice becomes a line in that file

Fourth, make it explain, then explain it back. Out loud, tab closed, to nobody. The moment you cannot, stop. You have just found the exact thing to go and learn, and it is worth more than the feature was. Fifth, every correction you give twice becomes a line in that file. That is how the brief writes itself. The second time you correct a machine about the same thing, it has stopped being a conversation and started being a rule.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-point | I don't prompt and pray. I prompt and prove.

Professional. It just means I keep the receipts. Get so good at the fundamentals that when you finally pick up the power tool, it multiplies something real. Bring nothing and it multiplies nothing. Ten times zero is still zero. So become a bigger number first, then go make it dangerous. I don't prompt and pray. I prompt and prove.

**Digest:** Run the AI, do not work for it. Start with a project you know, a brief file and cheap gates. Use AI to buy reps, not just output.

**Trial:** Write the first three lines of a brief file for a project you know: what it is, how it is built, and one thing not to touch.
