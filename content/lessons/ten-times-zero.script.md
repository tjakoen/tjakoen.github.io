# Ten Times Zero Is Still Zero

Status: DRAFT script for Tjakoen to read aloud and edit. Written from the note; not yet read or checked by him.

## Stop 1: I was the zero

You know the tone people use for vibe coder. Someone who types a wish into a chat box, pastes whatever comes back, and ships a house of cards they could not explain if you held their coffee hostage. Prompt and pray. No tests, no idea, no receipts.

I call myself one anyway. I just add a word in front. Professional. And because professional is the kind of claim that should arrive with evidence, I did the one thing the label almost never does. I counted. We will get to the numbers. First, the belief they are proving, because it fits on a napkin.

AI does not add to what you can do. It multiplies it. And ten times zero is still zero.

Give a strong developer an AI and you get a frighteningly fast strong developer. Give a beginner the same AI and you get a beginner who ships bugs faster and cannot tell which ones they are. Same tool. Wildly different results, because the tool multiplied what was already there.

That is why just use AI is terrible advice for a beginner, and never use AI is terrible advice for a professional. The honest version sits in the middle, and it has an order of operations. Become worth multiplying first.

If you open the note, there is a figure you can drag. Take it to zero. The output does not get smaller. It disappears.

Now, the part I do not usually lead with. For most of my twenties I was the zero. I was a talented amateur with just enough instinct to talk my way into rooms I had no business being in. Coding in school came easy, so I coasted on easy. Then I decided the real value was in business, and I kept running the same play. Say yes to running the thing before I actually knew how to run the thing.

It worked. That is the trap. It worked just enough to keep me doing it, and never enough to get me what I was after. I helped grow my family's little beachside campsite into a real resort, and then a pandemic flattened it. I ran marketing for a company until I learned I cannot stand doing marketing for anyone but myself. I started a cafe with friends, a salon with friends, and a marketing firm on my own. One broke even, one sold at break-even, one went nowhere. And it turns out you cannot tell your friends what to do. Then I built and launched an events platform, got it to a real product with real money moving, and watched it end badly.

Add it all up and it cost me real money and a few years I cannot get back. I do not regret a moment of it, because every one of those failures had the same thing in common. It was not the ideas. It was me. I kept starting things I was not the right person to start yet.

You have to be the right person before you try the thing. I paid the tuition so you do not have to.

So I did the unglamorous thing. I stopped chasing the next launch and worked on the one variable I could fix, which was me. I slowed down. I went back and learned the foundations properly, not just enough to pass. And I shoved myself into the parts I was worst at, like standing in front of a room full of students, or giving a talk on a stage.

That is what the word professional means to me. Not the title. The journey I had to take to be able to say any of this.

Next, I will tell you who is actually talking, and why that matters for everything after it.

**Digest:** AI multiplies what you bring, so ten times zero is still zero. I was the zero for years. Become worth multiplying first.

**Trial:** Pick one thing you would hand to an AI this week. Write down honestly whether you could check its answer without it. If not, that is the foundation to lay first.

## Stop 2: A decade of rehearsal

I told you that backstory to buy one sentence. I could have written every line of this project by hand.

I run a team of developers for a living. I teach software engineering part time, on a platform I built. And the thing serving you this note runs on a framework and a stack I built from the ground up. A side note on that, since people ask. Going without a framework was not ignorance of the alternatives. I lived inside frameworks for years. I walked away for my own projects because the platform the web is written on grew up while we were not looking, and I would rather build on the thing than on the thing on top of the thing.

None of that is there to impress you. It is there to place me on the multiplier. A beginner vibe codes because they cannot write the code. I vibe code because I can. I have learned that the typing was never the valuable part. The value is knowing what to build, how it should be shaped, and catching the machine in the act when it has quietly done something dumb. I have made those same mistakes by hand, expensively, enough times to smell them coming.

So when I hand the typing to a machine, I am not skipping the hard part. I did the hard part years ago, the boring way and the expensive way.

And while I am being honest, for the record: I do not hold a single credential in AI. I keep up with the field. What I have instead is a long list of ways I have been wrong, and I think that has taught me more than a syllabus would. I still fully intend to go and earn the real thing. For now, call it the next foundation I have not laid yet.

Here is what surprised me. Directing an AI was not a new skill I had to go and learn. It is the skill my whole twenties turned out to be building. The ventures that failed, the team I run, the classroom. I had been rehearsing for a decade without knowing what for.

Think about what a dev manager does. You hand work to people whose hands you do not control, you give them enough context to succeed, and then you read what comes back and catch the problems before they ship. A tech lead sets the conventions and keeps a room full of people pulling the same way. An educator explains a thing clearly enough that someone with zero context can do it, then checks the work and corrects the misunderstanding, not just the wrong answer. Now describe running an AI. It is the same list, top to bottom.

So I stopped treating the model like a magic oracle or a fancy search box. I started treating it like what it actually resembles. A fast, capable, occasionally overconfident junior on my team. Or a student rushing through an activity just to get it done. The moment I framed it that way I got better at it overnight, because I already knew how to do that.

Some evenings I have five to ten Claude sessions open at once, and it feels exactly like running a team. Delegate the work, keep each one productive, and stitch what comes out into something that works. When I read what they produce, I ask the same questions I ask of a junior's pull request or a student's submission. Does this meet the objectives? Does it understand what it shipped? Is it in line with our standards and our architecture?

And my one rule for students turns out to be my one rule for the machine. If you can't explain it, you didn't build it.

I am not learning a new skill with AI. I am spending the one I already paid for.

Next, the receipts. I said I counted, so let us look at what I found.

**Digest:** I can write the code, which is why I let the machine type it. Running an AI uses the same skills as managing, leading and teaching. If you can't explain it, you didn't build it.

**Trial:** Write down one role you have already held, a job, a club, a group project, and list two moves from it that you could reuse when directing an AI.

## Stop 3: The receipts

Everything in this stop comes straight from the git history of the thing you are reading this on. My portfolio, built on three tools I made for it. My own no-build framework, called BATCH. My own design system, called GRAIN. And a little Markdown CMS called MILL that renders these very words into the page.

It is not a demo, and it is not a tutorial repo. It is the thing I actually build on, and it is live. The main counts here were measured on the twelfth of November 2026, from committed files only, because those are the ones you can go and check. If I counted my working tree as well, where the plans and some agent notes live on purpose without being committed, I would get a friendlier number. I am not using it.

So here is the headline, because it reframes everything else. More than half of this repo is writing, not code. Seventy five thousand five hundred lines of words, roughly, against about fifty seven thousand lines of code. Ask a room to guess and they say twenty percent.

Sit with that, because it is the opposite of what vibe coding is supposed to look like. The caricature is a person who writes no docs, who could not produce a spec if you begged. This repo has the inverse problem. There is more specification than implementation.

I did not tidy that up after the fact. It is the method. When you work with an AI at speed, the scarce resource stops being code and becomes intent. The model cannot know what you meant, what the rules are, or what done looks like, unless you wrote it down. So I write it down. The documentation is the work. The code is the cheap part the machine handles.

One honest wrinkle, since I keep the receipts either way. This number wanders. My first count read fifty six percent. By August it had sagged to fifty two, for a boring reason. The stack moved into its own repositories and took a big slice of its documentation with it, while the site's code kept growing. I half expected it to keep sliding. It went the other way. Three months later it reads fifty seven percent. I did not set out to write more. The loop got stricter, and a stricter loop is mostly more things written down. The ratio moved. The habit did not.

Now, if all that writing makes me sound slow and fussy, the tempo says otherwise. When I counted in August, this repo had 503 commits, and every single one was co-authored with an AI. That does not mean the machine wrote while I watched. I still write plenty of the code myself. Co-authored is the honest word. Two sets of hands on every commit. Across the seven repositories I keep, there were 1,246 commits between the twenty seventh of June and the twenty second of August. Eight weeks.

One night in the middle of that, the fourth of July, I made fifty commits between nine in the evening and half past three in the morning, and added more than four thousand lines before it got light. One commit every eight minutes or so. That is vibe coding at full tilt.

But the commits were small on purpose. Each one is a thing that already works, and that is what makes the next one cheap to check. Fifty tiny commits you can verify beat five big ones you cannot.

Both things are true at once, and that is the point. Fast and documented. The reason I can sprint all night and not wake up to a pile of nonsense is not that I am careful instead of fast. It is that I did the careful part first. I built the rails before I opened the throttle.

Next, what those rails actually are, one rule at a time.

**Digest:** More than half of what I have written in this repo is words, not code. Writing down intent is the method. Speed is safe because the rails went up first.

## Stop 4: The playbook

Whether you use AI has nothing to do with whether you are professional. Neither does how fast you type. Amateur vibe coding is unstructured improvisation. Professional vibe coding is structured delegation. Same tools, same speed, wildly different amounts of scaffolding around the model.

The clearest way to see it is to split the job in two. The AI can generate a thousand lines an hour. It cannot decide the layering rule, the token architecture, the single write door, or what done means. Those were mine. The machine typed. I engineered.

I do not make the AI reliable by writing cleverer one-off prompts. I make it reliable by building it an environment where good work is the path of least resistance. That environment is a system, and you can copy it. It comes in seven rules.

Rule one. Write the rails before the features. Before I built much of anything, I wrote the rulebook. A conventions doc for how things are built and tested, and an onboarding doc written for the AI, so any model or human joining the project reads the same here is how this works, here is what not to touch, before it writes a line. You do not tell a fast driver to be careful. You build the track.

Rule two. Give the AI a memory. The model forgets everything between sessions, so I keep a running set of decision notes, about a hundred small records by now, of why things are the way they are. Next session the AI inherits the reasoning instead of relitigating it, or worse, quietly undoing it.

Rule three. Keep a sync map. The failure of fast AI work is usually not bad code. It is drift. You change one thing and six related things silently fall out of step. So I keep an explicit table: when you change this, also update that. It turns do not forget into a checklist a machine can follow.

Rule four. Audit relentlessly. A model earns more scrutiny than a junior, not less, because it is fluent enough to sound right exactly when it is wrong. Most of my prompting is not build this. It is check this. Does it still match the goal? Did it quietly break a rule to get something working? That is where most of the hours go. And one rule I would hand a student before any tooling. The second pass has to be done by someone who did not write the code. A session that just wrote something already believes it. It is the same reason you do not mark your own exam.

Rule five. Tests are part of the work, not a phase after it. The type checker and the test suite have to be green before I call anything done. It is the least glamorous rule and it does the most work. It is the seatbelt that lets me drive fast.

Rule six. Document for the machine, on purpose. The AI is a first-class reader, so I write for it, not around it. You onboard this collaborator every time you open a new session, so make the onboarding excellent.

Rule seven. Make the rails enforce themselves. Everything above is a habit, and habits are exactly what a tired person skips at two in the morning. I am the tired person. So the rules moved out of my head and into machinery. Every session opens with a checklist printed by a script, not recited by the model. Hooks refuse the moves I have already been burned by. None of it held up the first time. The check I trusted most ran once in 165 sessions, and I got the reason wrong before I got it right. A rule you have to remember is a wish. A rule that refuses the commit is a rail.

Two of these ideas have proper names, and I did not invent either. Context engineering is the first. Your prompt is not the input. The model reads the room: the files, the conventions, whatever the last session left behind. I tried fixing bad answers with cleverer prompts for months. Then I stopped prompting harder and started leaving better notes. Alfonso Graziano's book, Learning AI-Native Software Engineering, is where most of my thinking on this got sharpened. Loop engineering is the second, and it is Addy Osmani's term: durable AI work is built from a few reusable parts, not one clever prompt.

The loop I run for every change is four moves. Brief it. Build it. Verify it. Record what changed. Verify is doing all the work. Skip it once and nothing happens, which is the trap. Skip it for a week and neither you nor the machine can answer a question about your own project.

Next, the move I am proudest of, where the AI's mistakes become the measurement.

**Digest:** Professional vibe coding is structured delegation. Seven rules build the environment, and the last one makes them enforce themselves. Brief, build, verify, record.

**Trial:** Open a cold AI session with no history, give it one sentence asking for a small change to a project of yours, and note what it guesses wrong first. That gap is a note you have not written yet.

## Stop 5: The mistakes are a measurement

Here is the move I am proudest of. While I was building one of GRAIN's showcase pages, I kept making small mistakes. The grain texture not showing on the AI's text. A chat that rendered as two stacked boxes instead of a conversation. An AI click on a Send button that did not look like a click. Normal bug-fixing territory. The boring move is to squash each one and move on.

Instead we stopped, the machine and I, and started asking questions. Why do I keep tripping on my own system? Am I the problem? Is the architecture I planned the problem? I designed this thing to be easy for a human to use, never mind an AI. So why is it tripping on trivial things?

It is the same reflex I use reviewing code or grading a project. When the same mistake keeps coming back, you stop blaming the person making it and start suspecting the thing they are using. When half a class flubs the same question, the problem is the question, not the class.

So the mistakes stopped being bugs and started being data.

We ran a proper review, and the mistakes were not random. Every one of them clustered in one failure mode, which I call silent-failure contracts. Mechanisms that quietly do nothing when you use them slightly wrong. No error, no warning. The documented way just does not take. That is about the most dangerous kind of design there is. A thing that fails loudly, you fix. A thing that fails silently, you ship.

The AI surfaced it in a way I could not have alone, because an AI working from your docs is the toughest test of whether they are clear. It has no tribal knowledge. Nobody told it how everyone really does it. So when it trips on your system, that is not the machine being dumb. It is a free audit telling you that a tired human on a Friday will trip on exactly the same spot. Read the signal instead of just correcting the output.

So we did not document around the traps, and we did not bolt on a test to catch them later. We designed them out. We made the grain work on any element, and shipped the chat as a container so messages cannot misalign. Can't be done wrong beats we'll catch it if it is. Then we backed that up with tests that check the design system is used correctly, not merely that the page renders. One of them proves that a human action and an AI action produce the exact same result. And every fix was proven against the real thing, with screenshots and real end-to-end runs, never it probably looks right.

The one-sentence version. Where the AI keeps slipping is a map of where my design is not clear yet, and I harden the design so neither of us slips there again.

Now strip all of it down and you land back at the first number, the writing. About as much spec as implementation, in a repo whose whole reputation is supposed to be the opposite.

An amateur and I could type the exact same request into the exact same model and get back the exact same code. The difference is not in that moment. It is in everything I built around it. The conventions the model read first, the memory it inherited, the tests it has to pass, the audit it has to survive. That surrounding structure is the profession. The code is just the code.

It is the same law as the napkin at the start. The model multiplied what I brought. I brought conventions, memory, tests and taste, so it multiplied those.

Next, the one idea I would keep if you forgot everything else: do not work for the AI. Run it.

**Digest:** When the AI keeps slipping in the same place, the design is unclear there. Design the mistake out, then lock it with a test. The structure around the model is the profession.

**Trial:** Think of one mistake you or an AI made twice in the same project. Write one sentence on what about the project made it easy to make, and one change that would make it impossible.

## Stop 6: Run it, and where to start

If you take one idea out of all of this, take this one, because it decides which of two developers you become.

Working for the AI looks like this. You ask, it answers, you paste. The shape of the project ends up being whatever the machine happened to produce that afternoon. When something goes wrong you ask again, louder. And you spend a lot of your day sitting in front of one chat window, waiting.

Running it looks different from the first minute, before a single prompt gets typed. You decide what should exist. You cut it into pieces small enough to check in a minute each. You point the machine at one piece and read every line that comes back. And you accept nothing you couldn't defend with the tab closed.

Same tool, same day, same model. One of those is a job. The other is a habit that does not survive contact with anything real.

So where do you start, if you are early? In this order, because everybody wants to start at step three.

First, pick a project you already understand. Not a new one. You cannot judge a machine's output on a codebase you have never read, and judging the output is the entire skill.

Second, write the brief before the feature. One file at the root: what this is, how it is built, what not to touch. Twenty minutes, once. Every session after it starts smarter.

Third, turn on the cheap gates. Strict types, one test that actually runs, no empty catch blocks. All of that before lunch, and it keeps catching things all year.

Fourth, make it explain, then explain it back. Out loud, tab closed, to nobody. The moment you cannot, stop. You have just found the exact thing to go and learn, and it is worth more than the feature was.

Fifth, every correction you give twice becomes a line in that file. That is how the brief writes itself. The second time you correct a machine about the same thing, it has stopped being a conversation and started being a rule.

If you are early in your career and you use AI to produce, you are buying output. If you use it to learn, you are buying reps. Same hour. Only one of those makes you a bigger number.

So yes, I am a vibe coder. I let the machine write nearly everything, I work in fast overnight bursts, and I will tell anyone who asks that this is the most productive I have ever been. But I can also hand you the receipts, the commits, the ratio, the runbook, and show you it was not luck.

That is the whole word. Professional. It just means I keep the receipts. Get so good at the fundamentals that when you finally pick up the power tool, it multiplies something real.

Bring nothing and it multiplies nothing. Ten times zero is still zero. So become a bigger number first, then go make it dangerous.

I don't prompt and pray. I prompt and prove.

That is the end of this quest. Do the trial, save at the campfire, and press Continue for the next stop on the path.
