# Small parts, big screens

Status: DRAFT script for Tjakoen to read aloud and edit. Not yet read or checked by him.

Same format as the Harbor script: one idea a beat, about 20 to 45 seconds, names the phrase in the note that lights up, and adds the story, the why or the practice instead of repeating the page. This is a journey and an invitation, not a tutorial. Five words from a chemistry class, why they help when an AI does the typing, and how to borrow the question without building a design system.

## Stop 1: Five words from a chemistry class

**Beat 1**
highlight: intro | By the fortieth you have forty buttons

Here is the problem. Ask an AI for a button, then ask again next week in a new session, and you get a different button. It has no memory of the first. By the fortieth you have forty of them, and one bug to fix forty times. That is not carelessness. It is a teammate nobody told where the parts live.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-chemistry-class | every website is made from the same short list of HTML elements

Brad Frost is a designer who wrote a free book on this. He went looking for a deliberate order to build interfaces in, and remembered his chemistry class. Atoms make molecules, molecules make organisms. His point is that every website uses the same short list of HTML elements, so interfaces can be built the same way.

**Beat 3**
highlight: the-chemistry-class | A label, an input and a button become a search form

Hold on to one example. On their own, a label, an input and a button do nothing. Put them together and you have something that searches. That is a molecule. Put that search form in a header with a logo and a navigation and you have an organism.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-chemistry-class | figure: Atomic design in five levels

The five levels run from atoms to pages. Frost is clear that this is a way of thinking and not a procedure. You do not walk through it in order, you use it to ask what the parts are.

**Digest:** Ask an AI for a button ten times and you own ten buttons. | Brad Frost named five levels: atoms, molecules, organisms, templates and pages. | A label, an input and a button become a search form, and that is a molecule.

**Trial:** Name the atoms in one page you use every day, such as a button, a field and a link.

**Runes:**
intro | By the fortieth you have forty buttons
the-chemistry-class | A label, an input and a button become a search form

## Stop 2: Why it helps an AI do the typing

**Beat 1**
highlight: why-this-helps-when-an-ai-does-the-typing | Use the existing button atom

An instruction with a level in it is harder to misread. Use the existing button atom tells the agent where the part lives. Add a button tells it nothing, and you get a new one.

**Beat 2**
highlight: why-this-helps-when-an-ai-does-the-typing | you fix the atom

The second habit is about fixing. When something is wrong everywhere, you fix the atom, and every molecule and organism built from it is fixed. A new color is an atom, a new field is a molecule, a new section is an organism.

**Beat 3**
highlight: what-it-looks-like-in-my-own-system | each one lives in exactly one of them

This is how my own design system, GRAIN, is laid out. Its components sit in folders called atoms, molecules and organisms, and each one lives in exactly one. A stat tile is built from atoms. A dashboard is built from stat tiles.

**Beat 4**
highlight: what-it-looks-like-in-my-own-system | The atoms stay simple, the molecules carry the meaning, and the organisms just arrange them.

There is a rule in there that shows why the levels matter. The atom that sets the text face knows nothing about who wrote the text. A molecule, like a message bubble, is what marks itself as written by the AI. The organisms just arrange the result.

**Digest:** Name the level and your instruction has a place in it. | Fix an atom and everything built from it is fixed too. | In GRAIN each component lives in exactly one of the atoms, molecules and organisms folders.

**Trial:** Ask your AI to name the level of three things on your portfolio and say which one it would fix first if the color changed.

**Runes:**
why-this-helps-when-an-ai-does-the-typing | Use the existing button atom
what-it-looks-like-in-my-own-system | each one lives in exactly one of them

## Stop 3: Borrow the question, not my files

**Beat 1**
highlight: you-dont-need-a-design-system-yet | ask what the atoms are

I am not telling you to build a design system. For one portfolio page it would be a lot of ceremony. Borrow the question. Ask what the atoms are, a date, a title, a link. What the molecule is, one roadmap entry. And what the organism is, the list.

**Beat 2**
highlight: you-dont-need-a-design-system-yet | make the entry once

Then tell your AI to make the entry once and use it for every row. That sentence alone saves you the forty buttons.

**Beat 3**
highlight: your-turn | The five levels are a way of talking, and a blurry one.

And where I might be wrong. People argue about whether something is a molecule or an organism, and for a small project the argument costs more than it saves. Use the words while they make your instructions clearer. Drop them the day they do not.

**Digest:** You do not need a design system yet. Borrow the question. | Ask what the atoms, the molecule and the organism of your page are. | The five levels are a blurry way of talking. Drop them the day they stop helping.

**Trial:** Ask your AI to label your roadmap wireframe in the five levels, fix one thing you disagree with, then have it build one roadmap entry as a single reusable piece.

**Runes:**
you-dont-need-a-design-system-yet | ask what the atoms are
your-turn | The five levels are a way of talking, and a blurry one.
