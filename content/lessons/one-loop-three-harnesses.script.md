# One Loop, Three Harnesses

Status: DRAFT script for Tjakoen to read aloud and edit. Cut into beats from the earlier narration; not yet read or checked by him.

How this script works. The note stays open on the page while the recording plays. Each beat is a short spoken line about one idea, and it names the phrase in the note that lights up while it plays. The wording is the earlier narration, cut to the lines that carry the idea. Format of a beat: a highlight line (section id, then a quote that must appear in that section of the note, or figure: and a figure name), then the spoken words.

## Stop 1: The day Claude ran out

**Beat 1**
highlight: intro | deleted the DeepSeek Harness app, and DeepSeek

Since I wrote it I deleted the DeepSeek Harness app. DeepSeek now reaches me through OpenCode, next to free models and the Codex plan, behind a layer that picks the model for me. And the privacy line you'll hear about later became a hard rule, enforced by guards. What follows is how it looked when I wrote it.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-day-one-of-them-ran-out | opened Codex, checked out the same branch, and finished

So I tried Codex. I'd heard good things, and it had a free month. I used it when Claude ran out. The first time that happened in the middle of a task, I opened Codex, checked out the same branch, and finished the work without changing a single instruction. That should have felt like freedom. It exposed a problem instead. Moving between providers in the middle of a project is not smooth unless something holds the state. That was the day I stopped thinking of the model as the thing I was buying.

**Beat 3**
highlight: the-day-one-of-them-ran-out | What I am buying is usage, not access.

So here is my setup. Three devices, two subscriptions, one usage account, and a private repo for my AI settings. On the entry plans I still get the top model. What I'm buying is usage, not access. The usage is the cheap part. The loop is the expensive part, and I built it myself.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-loop-was-the-thing-i-had-not-written-down | A loop that is remembered is not a loop.

That note carries a correction. Three weeks after I published it, I found the doctor, the check that is supposed to start every session, had run once in a hundred and sixty-five sessions. The script couldn't find its own program from any folder but the one beside it. It said it was unavailable and quietly exited. I left the claim and the correction both in public, because the gap is the useful part. A loop that is remembered is not a loop. It has to be checked.

**Digest:** Running out of Claude mid-task showed that switching models only works if something holds the state. The model is rented, and the loop is built. A loop you only remember is not a loop.

**Trial:** Write down where the state of your current project lives right now: the plan, the branch, the next step. If it only lives in your head or one chat, note that.

## Stop 2: The model is a route

**Beat 1**
highlight: what-the-loop-actually-is | A plans folder is enough; it is just what I use.

So what is the loop? A plan is a markdown file. The board is a window on those files, not a database. And you don't need PANTRY. A folder of plans is enough. It's just what I use. The doctor runs first and puts what's due in front of me. A run leaves a report with the checks it actually ran. That's five pieces. One instruction file, one plan, one checklist, one report and a human gate.

**Beat 2**
highlight: three-harnesses-one-standard | The rules did not change.

Every rule in my loop standards assumes something is running the hooks, and for a long time I didn't say what. Then I started moving between harnesses, and the loop had to prove it wasn't the harness. In Everybody Wants the Agent it meant the scaffolding around a model: what it sees, what it may touch, who checks the result. Here I mean the program that runs the session. Claude Code, Codex, DeepSeek Harness. The loop sits above both meanings. I use all three. The first two get the loop through a settings file. DeepSeek got it through a small plugin. The rules did not change.

**Beat 3**
highlight: the-model-is-a-routing-decision | entry window is enough. Two entry plans plus

A plan is not a model. It's the size of the window, and the entry window is enough. Two entry plans plus the cheap DeepSeek meter cost me less and do more than the big plan on one. I won't quote numbers. The shape is what matters. I max the allowance out on purpose, so I don't feel like I'm wasting money. A fixed window is predictable, and it forces me to choose the work. An open-ended token meter makes me hesitate, and hesitation is a tax.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-model-is-a-routing-decision | The model switch is not a crisis. It is a Tuesday.

I pick an owner model per project. The owner is the one I reach for first, not a lock. I've pushed one too hard, hit the five-hour or the weekly window, and moved the same project to the other. The loop is what makes that safe. The work is committed, the main branch is clean, the work sits on its own branch, and the handoff says what it is and where it stops. That's the part I didn't expect. The model switch is not a crisis. It's a Tuesday.

**Digest:** The loop is five pieces: an instruction file, a plan, a checklist, a report and a human gate. The harness and the model can change underneath it. Switching is safe because the work is committed and handed off.

**Trial:** Write a three-line handoff for something you're working on: what the work is, where it stops, and which branch it's on. Could another model pick it up from that alone?

**Runes:**

three-harnesses-one-standard | The rules did not change.

## Stop 3: The line I do not cross

**Beat 1**
highlight: a-week-with-one-to-five-projects | hand it off and go do something else.

Outside work I run one to five projects at a time. The big one is the next course platform. Last semester's native course platform proved the shape works, and this one is the proper hosted platform. I wouldn't have started it without the loop. The loop is good enough that when an idea arrives, I can hand it off and go do something else. That's what changed my week, not the model. And this isn't a flex about volume. I do a lot with AI because the loop makes the volume safe to carry, not because I can hold it all in my head. I can't.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-line-i-do-not-cross | The separation is the machine and the account, not the room.

I work from home, so the work laptop sits in the same room as the personal machines. The separation is the machine and the account, not the room. Work projects stay on the work laptop and in company accounts. Personal projects stay on the personal machines. The work devices have security that would flag or block a personal project before it got far. That separation isn't a disclaimer. It's what lets me use one loop for both without lying about which is which. I use it for work that's already public or disposable, and for experiments where cost matters. Never for student data, employer code, or anything private.

**Beat 3**
highlight: what-i-have-not-solved | It cannot prove the work is good.

The checklist can prove the loop loaded. It cannot prove the work is good. The second reader is still the thing that catches the work. This is one person's setup, not a study, and tool preference is not evidence. The private config is one point of failure. If it breaks, all three machines break. But it's not three drifting copies. Each machine links to the same config, and the checklist catches a machine that has slipped.

**Beat 4**
highlight: the-part-i-keep | I am loyal to the loop that lets me switch.

The plans will change, and so will the models. The loop is the part I keep: the plan, the gate, the evidence, the human who lands it. I'm not loyal to a model. I'm loyal to the loop that lets me switch.

**Digest:** One loop carries five threads because it holds the state. Work and personal stay split by machine and account. The checklist proves the loop loaded, never that the work is good.

**Trial:** List the places your AI tools can see data. For each, write one line saying what must never go there.

**Runes:**
the-line-i-do-not-cross | The separation is the machine and the account, not the room.
what-i-have-not-solved | It cannot prove the work is good.
the-part-i-keep | I am loyal to the loop that lets me switch.

## Stop 4: One run in a hundred and sixty-five

**Beat 1**
highlight: one-run-in-a-hundred-and-sixty-five-sessions | Not one in ten. One.

On a Wednesday night, three weeks after I published a note about how well my workflow was going, I ran a query. A hundred and sixty-five AI sessions in my largest client project since early July. One of them ran the check that's supposed to fire at the start of every single one. Not one in ten. One. And I'd written, in those words, that I'd made every one of my repos run it. That sentence went out on the thirtieth of July. The number is from the nineteenth of August.

**Beat 2**
highlight: the-story-i-told-myself-and-it-was-a-good-one | which is a warning sign I did not read at the time

I didn't shrug. I took the numbers seriously and built an explanation, and it was about discipline. A check that fires when you sit down costs you right now. It interrupts, it hands you a list, it delays what you came to do. Whatever it buys you comes later, often for somebody else. In my case that's a future me who's forgotten why any of this is here. Cost now, payoff later and elsewhere. That machinery gets skipped. Every time. I still like it, which was a warning sign I didn't read.

**Beat 3**
highlight: then-i-ran-it-myself-from-the-wrong-folder | It could not find its own program.

Late that day I did what I should have done first. I opened the client project and ran the check by hand. It could not find its own program. Its job is to find my tooling and ask what's out of shape in the repo. Finding the tooling took three tries. So from anywhere but that directory, all three miss. The script prints a line saying the check didn't run, and it exits quietly with a success code.

**Beat 4**
highlight: then-i-ran-it-myself-from-the-wrong-folder | hundred and sixty-five sessions being told

Those hundred and sixty-five sessions weren't declining a check. They were being told it was unavailable, in a message so small that neither I nor the machine reading it ever treated it as a fault. The one recorded run was a human typing the command. That human was me, standing in the one folder where it works.

**Digest:** The check had run once in a hundred and sixty-five sessions. I built a discipline theory for it. Running it by hand showed it had never been able to start.

**Trial:** Pick one hook or script you rely on. Run it by hand from a folder other than the one you built it in, and read what it says.

## Stop 5: A path with two dots in it

**Beat 1**
highlight: why-the-wrong-answer-was-so-comfortable | There is only a path with two dots in it.

I spent a day on the wrong diagnosis, and it isn't because I'm careless with data. The wrong diagnosis was a story about behavior, and I already had a moral ready for it. A behavior story flatters you a little, even when it accuses you. It says the tool is sound and the human is weak, and I know how to fix that. More discipline, better nudges, a rule written down somewhere. A plumbing story says the tool never worked and you didn't check. There's no moral in that. There's only a path with two dots in it.

**Beat 2**
highlight: why-the-wrong-answer-was-so-comfortable | measurement tells you what did not happen.

A measurement tells you what didn't happen. It will never tell you why. The gap between those two is exactly the size of a confident wrong answer. Every number I had was correct. The reading I laid on top of them was invented. It survived three weeks because it was plausible and because nobody, me included, ran the one command that would have killed it in five seconds.

**Beat 3**
highlight: what-i-still-cannot-reproduce-including-one-i-got-wrong-twice | Measuring the loop is loop work.

The first is that I labelled a number wrong. The two hundred and eighty-eight is the sum of the four other repositories in my table. So there's a confession inside the confession. While writing up how I misread my measurement, I misread a different one, on the same page. On that night, fifty-three of the hundred and forty-eight sessions here were about the workflow, the standards, the tooling or an audit, not anything a visitor would ever see. Roughly a third of the work was the machinery, not the thing the machinery is for. Of the six since, five were about the workflow. Measuring the loop is loop work.

**Beat 4**
highlight: what-it-says-now | that is a rollout and not a result

I pointed it at the client project, and for the first time in a hundred and sixty-five sessions it answered. Twenty-one checks, none failing, three due. A stale code graph, a missing end to end suite, and a context budget I'd blown by seven thousand characters. Eleven repositories now carry the config and the instructions, and the check reaches all of them. And I have to stop there, because that's a rollout and not a result. Calling a rollout a result is what I got wrong last time. So it's a hypothesis with a date on it. If I run the same query in October and the four quiet repos are still quiet, my discipline theory was right and I owe it an apology. If they aren't, the check was simply never in the room.

**Digest:** A measurement says what did not happen, never why. The behavior story was comfortable, and the cause was a relative path. A rollout is not a result until it is measured again.

**Trial:** Take one number you have quoted about your own work. Write down the date it was true, and the one command that would confirm or kill your reading of it.

**Runes:**
why-the-wrong-answer-was-so-comfortable | There is only a path with two dots in it.
what-i-still-cannot-reproduce-including-one-i-got-wrong-twice | Measuring the loop is loop work.
what-it-says-now | that is a rollout and not a result
